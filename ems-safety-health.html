EMS occupational safety

A long career requires more than clinical skill.

EMS clinicians face patient-handling injuries, vehicle and roadway hazards, violence, infectious exposure, hazardous substances, fatigue, and behavioral-health strain. Treat prevention and reporting as patient-care competencies.

Review the hazardsBuild my safety plan
Immediate help: If you may hurt yourself or someone else, call emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department. In the United States, call or text 988 for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

Six hazards to plan for

Follow your employer's policies, training, exposure-control plan, PPE program, driving standards, and reporting process. This page is a planning aid, not an operational protocol.

Patient handling and injury

Use available lift equipment, request help early, plan the route, communicate the move, keep equipment maintained, and report pain or injury promptly.

Ambulance and roadway risk

Use restraints, minimize unrestrained patient-compartment work, drive for conditions, use high-visibility protection as required, and maintain situational awareness around traffic.

Violence and scene risk

Stage when indicated, identify exits, monitor escalating behavior, avoid preventable entrapment, request law enforcement according to policy, and document threats or assaults.

Infection and exposure

Use standard precautions, appropriate PPE, safe sharps practices, cleaning procedures, vaccinations offered through occupational health, and immediate exposure reporting.

Hazardous substances

Do not enter uncontrolled environments beyond your training and PPE. Use dispatch information, placards, monitoring, decontamination, and specialized resources according to local plans.

Fatigue and behavioral health

Track sleep impairment, near misses, mood changes, substance use, intrusive symptoms, and isolation. Seek qualified support before a pattern becomes a crisis.

Personal safety plan

Selections save only on this device. Review the plan after an injury, exposure, assault, near miss, schedule change, or new assignment.

After an exposure, injury, assault, or near miss

1

Control the immediate hazard

Move to safety, obtain patient or responder care, and request additional resources.

2

Notify and document

Use the agency's time-sensitive reporting route. Record objective facts while they are fresh.

3

Complete follow-up

Use occupational health, exposure protocols, medical evaluation, equipment review, and supervisor follow-up as applicable.

4

Learn without blame

Identify equipment, staffing, environment, communication, training, and system contributors—not only the final action.
