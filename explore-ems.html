Start before EMT school

Could EMS be right for you?

Learn what EMTs really do, compare the rewards and challenges, discover how to prepare during high school, and decide whether emergency medical services deserves a closer look.

Take the career-fit quizBuild your high-school plan

You do not need to decide today

This page is designed to help you explore—not to recruit you into a career that may not fit. EMS can be meaningful, exciting, exhausting, repetitive, stressful, funny, frustrating, and deeply human, sometimes during the same shift.

Requirements differ by state, employer, school, and age. Confirm local rules before enrolling, volunteering, completing a ride-along, or applying for work.

The honest version

What EMTs really do

EMTs assess sick and injured people, provide emergency care within their level of training, communicate with patients and other professionals, document care, move patients safely, and may transport patients to medical facilities.

Work people notice

  • Respond to medical emergencies and injuries
  • Perform patient assessments and take vital signs
  • Give oxygen, CPR, bleeding control, splinting, and other approved care
  • Work with paramedics, firefighters, nurses, police, dispatchers, and physicians
  • Comfort patients and families during frightening moments

Work people forget

  • Clean and disinfect equipment
  • Restock the ambulance and check supplies
  • Lift, carry, and move patients safely
  • Write patient-care reports
  • Wait at hospitals, post in an ambulance, and handle routine calls
  • Work nights, weekends, holidays, or long shifts

Career overview: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics—EMTs and Paramedics.

Interactive career explorer

Would you enjoy EMS?

Choose the answer that is most honest today. This is not a test and there is no failing result. Skills such as communication, confidence, fitness, and stress management can improve with practice.

1. How do you feel about talking with strangers?
2. When someone is upset, what happens to you?
3. How do you handle constructive correction?
4. Do you enjoy practical problem-solving?
5. How interested are you in medicine and the human body?
6. How do you feel about teamwork?
7. How do you react to blood, illness, or injury?
8. Are you willing to do routine work?
9. How flexible are you about schedules?
10. Are you willing to keep learning after certification?

Look at both sides

Reasons people choose EMS—and reasons some leave

Possible rewards

  • Work that directly helps people
  • A strong team environment
  • Variety and practical problem-solving
  • A relatively fast entry into patient care
  • A foundation for paramedicine, fire service, nursing, physician assistant education, emergency management, and other careers
  • Opportunities to serve your community

Possible challenges

  • Stressful or emotionally difficult calls
  • Physical demands and injury risk
  • Interrupted sleep and irregular schedules
  • Documentation and routine work
  • Pay that varies widely by region and employer
  • Need for continuing education, recertification, and ongoing feedback

Do not choose EMS only because you like action. Consider whether you also value patient care, teamwork, responsibility, communication, and steady improvement.

Good moves

Prepare

Use high school to build a foundation

Helpful classesScience + communication

Courses to consider

  • Biology, anatomy and physiology, health science, and medical terminology
  • English, speech, psychology, and classes that strengthen communication
  • Algebra and practical math
  • Physical education, strength, and safe movement

Activities to explore

HOSA, health-science or CTE programs, CPR/first aid courses, public-safety academies, cadet or Explorer programs, and supervised volunteer opportunities where locally available.

Get ready

Research real EMT programs

Before enrollingCompare carefully

Questions to answer

  • Does my state or program have a minimum age?
  • Is the course state approved?
  • What are the tuition, textbook, uniform, testing, background-check, and immunization costs?
  • Does my high school offer dual enrollment or CTE credit?
  • How are clinical and field experiences arranged?

Important distinction

National Registry certification and state permission to practice are not the same thing. State licensure or certification is required before legally working as an EMT.

Transition

Move from student to EMT candidate

Next destinationEMT roadmap

Typical sequence

  1. Meet local and program prerequisites.
  2. Complete a state-approved EMT education program.
  3. Complete required certification testing.
  4. Apply for state authorization to practice.
  5. Meet employer requirements and apply for work.

Do not rush

A cheaper or faster course is not automatically better. Compare instruction, lab time, support, clinical access, pass rates when available, and total cost.
