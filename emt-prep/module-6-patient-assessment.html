Patient Assessment Foundations

Preview scene size-up, primary assessment, history, focused assessment, vital signs, treatment, reassessment, and handoff.

Core lesson

1. Assessment is a repeating process

A useful mental map is scene size-up, primary assessment, history, focused or secondary assessment, vital signs, treatment, reassessment, handoff, and documentation. The process loops whenever the patient changes. Immediate threats are managed as they are found rather than postponed for a perfect checklist.

Core lesson

2. Scene size-up

Look for hazards, appropriate protective equipment, mechanism of injury or nature of illness, number of patients, need for extra resources, and clues in the environment. Scene safety is dynamic. A calm scene can become unsafe, and an apparently minor call can involve more patients or hazards than dispatch reported.

Core lesson

3. Primary assessment

Form a general impression, assess mental status, airway, breathing, circulation, major bleeding, and immediate threats. Decide whether the patient appears stable or time-sensitive and whether transport or additional resources should be accelerated. Your approved program will teach its exact sequence and terminology.

Core lesson

4. History and focused assessment

SAMPLE organizes symptoms, allergies, medications, pertinent history, last oral intake, and events. OPQRST explores onset, provocation/palliation, quality, region/radiation, severity, and time. Physical assessment uses inspection, palpation, auscultation where appropriate, comparisons, and relevant positives and negatives.

Core lesson

5. Reassessment and handoff

Reassessment confirms whether the patient changed and whether interventions helped, failed, or caused problems. A handoff should identify the patient, chief concern, important history, assessment findings, vital trends, treatment, response, and remaining concerns. Good reports are concise but not empty.

Applied activity · about 8–10 minutes

Put the lesson into practice

Use the fictional complaint “difficulty breathing.” Write your first five observations or questions and label each as scene, primary, history, or focused assessment.
