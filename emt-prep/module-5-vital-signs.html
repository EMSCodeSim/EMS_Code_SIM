Vital Signs Foundations

Understand what vital signs represent, how technique changes accuracy, and why trends matter more than isolated numbers.

85–110 minutesSaved progressPre-course readiness

Foundation only

Core lesson

1. Vital signs are observations, not answers

Vital signs provide a baseline and reveal trends. They must be interpreted with appearance, complaint, history, environment, age, medications, and treatment. A single normal value does not cancel a concerning presentation, and a single abnormal value should be confirmed when practical without delaying needed care.

Core lesson

2. Manual blood pressure

Understand systolic and diastolic pressure, cuff sizing, arm position, gauge visibility, bulb and valve control, palpated estimation, and auscultated measurement. A cuff that is too small can read high; one that is too large can read low. Movement, talking, poor positioning, rapid deflation, and noise can reduce accuracy.

App quick reference
  • For the website drill, mark adult BP normal at about 90–119 systolic and 60–79 diastolic.
  • Systolic is the first Korotkoff sound; diastolic is the disappearance of sounds in the adult practice method.
  • Document the reading, method, arm or position when relevant, and the patient context.

3. Pulse

Assess pulse rate, rhythm, and strength—not rate alone. Count long enough to recognize irregularity, use the correct pulse location for the situation, and describe the finding objectively.

App quick reference
  • Typical resting adult reference: about 60–100 BPM.
  • Assess and document rate, rhythm, quality or strength, and location.
  • A weak rapid pulse with cool, clammy skin changes the perfusion picture.

Core lesson

4. Respirations

Assess respiratory rate, rhythm, depth, effort, chest movement, speech, and associated sounds or signs. Respirations are often under-measured because providers announce the count or guess. Count quietly and extend the observation when the pattern is irregular.

App quick reference
  • Typical adult reference: about 12–20 breaths/min.
  • Document rate, rhythm, depth, effort, chest movement, speech, and associated sounds.
  • Do not announce the count; observe longer when the pattern is irregular.

Core lesson

5. Pulse oximetry

Pulse oximetry estimates oxygen saturation and pulse rate, but it does not directly measure ventilation. Motion, poor perfusion, sensor placement, nail coverings, dyshemoglobins, and device limitations can reduce reliability. Always compare the number with the patient.

App quick reference
  • Typical healthy-adult reference: about 95–100%.
  • Confirm a stable signal or waveform and compare the displayed pulse with a manual pulse.
  • Document percentage, room air or oxygen device and flow, respiratory effort, skin signs, mental status, and reassessment.

Core lesson

6. Skin signs

Describe skin color, temperature, and moisture rather than writing only “normal.” Skin findings add information about perfusion, environment, illness, and response to care, but they must be interpreted with the rest of the assessment.

App quick reference
  • Describe color, temperature, and moisture together.
  • Warm, pink, and dry is a typical description, but compare with baseline and skin tone.
  • Cool or clammy, hot or flushed, cyanotic, pale, or mottled findings require patient-context interpretation.

Core lesson

7. Blood glucose

A glucose value must be connected to mental status, symptoms, history, medications, food intake, meter quality, and local procedure. Verify that the sample and device process are appropriate, and document the actual result.

App quick reference
  • App practice bands: low below 70 mg/dL; reference band 70–180; high 181–350; critical or concerning above 350.
  • A normal BGL does not rule out stroke, overdose, sepsis, head injury, seizure, hypoxia, or other causes of altered mental status.
  • Never give anything by mouth when the patient cannot protect the airway or swallow safely.

Core lesson

8. Pupils and mental status

Pupil size, equality, shape, reactivity, gaze, and associated mental-status findings can add neurologic context. Describe what you observe without jumping directly to a diagnosis.

App quick reference
  • Pupils: document size in millimeters, equality, shape, reactivity, and patient-left versus patient-right.
  • AVPU: Alert, responds to Verbal, responds to Pain, or Unresponsive.
  • A&O×4: person, place, time, and event; document exactly which domains are present or absent.

9. Temperature

Temperature varies with measurement route, device, age, environment, and clinical condition. Record the actual value, units, time, route or device, and patient context rather than documenting only “fever” or “normal.”

Core lesson

10. Reassessment and documentation

Repeat vital signs after an intervention, a patient change, or according to local expectations. Record the actual value, time, method when relevant, and patient context. Trends such as rising respiratory rate, falling mental status, or improving work of breathing often matter more than a single isolated number.

Applied activity · about 8–10 minutes

Measure and document a complete basic vital-sign set

With a willing adult and only noninvasive methods you have been taught, practice pulse and respirations. Then write an objective entry that includes rate, rhythm, depth or effort, time, and factors that could reduce accuracy. Use the simulators for blood pressure, SpO₂, glucose, pupils, skin, and temperature until supervised skills training is available.

Knowledge check · about 7–10 minutes

Check your understanding

This is a readiness check, not a certification exam.

1. A single normal vital sign should be interpreted as

Vital signs are observations that gain meaning from appearance, complaint, history, trends, and response to care.

2. A blood pressure cuff that is too small may produce a reading that is

A cuff that is too small can overestimate blood pressure.

3. A complete pulse assessment includes

Pulse quality includes rate, rhythm, and strength, along with location and patient context.

4. Which factor can make pulse-oximetry less reliable?

Motion, poor perfusion, placement problems, nail coverings, and device limitations can affect readings.

5. Which trend is most concerning?

Worsening work of breathing and mental status can signal respiratory failure even before other values become extreme.

6. A complete respiratory observation includes which group of findings?

Respiratory assessment includes rate, rhythm, depth, effort, chest movement, speech, and associated sounds or signs.

7. Why should the temperature measurement route or device be documented?

Temperature readings can vary by route and device, so the method belongs with the value and patient context.

8. A patient opens the eyes only after a painful stimulus. AVPU is

A response only to a painful stimulus is classified as P.
