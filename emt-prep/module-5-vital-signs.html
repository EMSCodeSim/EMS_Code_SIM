How to Take and Document Vital Signs

Build the actual measurement routine: prepare, position, measure, verify, interpret, document, and reassess using guided practice and matching simulators.

150–190 minutesSaved progressPre-course readiness

Foundation only

This module prepares you to begin approved EMT education. It does not provide certification, course credit, local protocols, medical direction, or authorization to perform patient care.

Estimated total: 150–190 minutes

Learning objectives

  • Perform the preparation, positioning, measurement, verification, and documentation sequence for each vital sign.
  • Recognize common technique errors before they create a misleading result.
  • Use the matching simulator immediately after learning each skill.
  • Document a complete objective vital-sign set and identify meaningful trends.
  • Know which skills require approved equipment, instructor supervision, or local procedure.

Vital signs are a hands-on assessment skill

Numbers matter only when the technique is reliable and the result is connected to the patient. In this module, every vital sign follows the same field-ready pattern:

1 Prepare2 Position3 Measure4 Verify5 Document6 Reassess

Practice boundary: Use the simulators for all skills. Practice only noninvasive observation with a willing adult. Blood glucose, painful stimuli, and patient-care procedures belong in approved instruction with appropriate PPE, equipment, and supervision.

Reassessment and trends

Short final check · about 7–10 minutes

Confirm the skills you just practiced

The main learning happens in the eight skill labs. Use this check to identify any technique you should repeat.

1. A single normal vital sign should be interpreted as

Vital signs are observations that gain meaning from appearance, complaint, history, trends, and response to care.

2. A blood pressure cuff that is too small may produce a reading that is

A cuff that is too small can overestimate blood pressure.

3. A complete pulse assessment includes

Pulse quality includes rate, rhythm, and strength, along with location and patient context.

4. Which factor can make pulse-oximetry less reliable?

Motion, poor perfusion, placement problems, nail coverings, and device limitations can affect readings.

5. Which trend is most concerning?

Worsening work of breathing and mental status can signal respiratory failure even before other values become extreme.

6. A complete respiratory observation includes which group of findings?

Respiratory assessment includes rate, rhythm, depth, effort, chest movement, speech, and associated sounds or signs.

7. Why should the temperature measurement route or device be documented?

Temperature readings can vary by route and device, so the method belongs with the value and patient context.

8. A patient opens the eyes only after a painful stimulus. AVPU is

A response only to a painful stimulus is classified as P.

Technique references and course authority

The skill labs reinforce common adult measurement principles such as correct cuff fit, arm support near heart level, using two fingers for a radial pulse, waiting for a stable pulse-ox signal, and documenting measurement context. Your approved EMT instructor, equipment manufacturer, medical direction, and local procedure remain the final authority.
