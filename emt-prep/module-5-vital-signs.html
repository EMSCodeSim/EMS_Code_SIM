Vital Signs Foundations

Understand what vital signs represent, how technique changes accuracy, and why trends matter more than isolated numbers.

70–90 minutesSaved progressPre-course readiness

Foundation only

This module prepares you to begin approved EMT education. It does not provide certification, course credit, local protocols, medical direction, or authorization to perform patient care.

Learning objectives

  • Recognize common terms, observations, or equipment connected to the topic.

Core lesson

1. Vital signs are observations, not answers

Vital signs provide a baseline and reveal trends. They must be interpreted with appearance, complaint, history, environment, age, medications, and treatment. A single normal value does not cancel a concerning presentation, and a single abnormal value should be confirmed when practical without delaying needed care.

Core lesson

2. Manual blood pressure

Understand systolic and diastolic pressure, cuff sizing, arm position, gauge visibility, bulb and valve control, palpated estimation, and auscultated measurement. A cuff that is too small can read high; one that is too large can read low. Movement, talking, poor positioning, rapid deflation, and noise can reduce accuracy.

Core lesson

3. Pulse and respirations

Pulse assessment includes rate, rhythm, and strength. Respiratory assessment includes rate, rhythm, depth, effort, chest movement, speech, and associated sounds or signs. Counting should be long enough to capture irregularity. Respirations are often under-measured because providers announce the count or guess.

Core lesson

4. SpO₂, skin, glucose, and temperature

Pulse oximetry estimates oxygen saturation but can be affected by motion, poor perfusion, sensor placement, nail coverings, dyshemoglobins, and device limitations. Skin color, temperature, and moisture add perfusion context. Glucose values must be connected to symptoms and meter quality. Temperature route and device affect interpretation.

Core lesson

5. Reassessment and documentation

Repeat vital signs after an intervention, a change, or according to local expectations. Record the actual value, time, method when relevant, and patient context. Trends such as rising respiratory rate, falling mental status, or improving work of breathing often matter more than a single isolated number.

Applied activity · about 8–10 minutes

Put the lesson into practice

Practice measuring a pulse and respirations on a willing adult without providing medical advice. Record rate, rhythm, effort, and what could reduce accuracy.

Knowledge check · about 7–10 minutes

Check your understanding

This is a readiness check, not a certification exam.

1. A single normal vital sign should be interpreted as

Vital signs are observations that gain meaning from appearance, complaint, history, trends, and response to care.

2. A blood pressure cuff that is too small may produce a reading that is

A cuff that is too small can overestimate blood pressure.

3. A complete pulse assessment includes

Pulse quality includes rate, rhythm, and strength, along with location and patient context.

4. Which factor can make pulse-oximetry less reliable?

Motion, poor perfusion, placement problems, nail coverings, and device limitations can affect readings.

5. Which trend is most concerning?

Worsening work of breathing and mental status can signal respiratory failure even before other values become extreme.
