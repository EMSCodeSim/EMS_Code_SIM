About 7 minutes

1. Medical words are built in pieces

Medical terminology becomes easier when you stop treating every term as a separate vocabulary item. Many words are assembled from reusable parts.

PrefixAppears at the beginning and often describes location, amount, speed, or timing.Example: brady- = slow
RootCarries the main meaning, often a body structure or concept.Example: cardi = heart
SuffixAppears at the end and often describes a condition, process, or specialty.Example: -itis = inflammation

A combining vowel—often o—may connect a root to another root or suffix. In cardiology, cardi refers to the heart, o connects the parts, and -logy means study of.

A five-step decoding method

  1. Look at the suffix first.
  2. Find the root or roots.
  3. Identify any prefix.
  4. Translate each part into plain language.
  5. Rebuild the meaning in a natural order and check the clinical context.

About 6 minutes

3. Use anatomical language to remove ambiguity

Position

Supine means lying face up. Prone means lying face down. Fowler position generally describes an upright or semi-upright posture.

Direction

Anterior/posterior, superior/inferior, medial/lateral, and proximal/distal describe relationships between structures.

Sides and planes

Right and left refer to the patient’s sides. Sagittal, frontal, and transverse planes describe how the body can be divided.

Example: “A laceration is on the lateral surface of the right forearm, distal to the elbow” is more precise than “the cut is on the outside of the arm.” Precise language helps another clinician visualize the finding without seeing it.

Translate these phrases

  • Anterior chest
  • Distal left leg
  • Medial right ankle
  • Posterior neck
Show suggested translations

Front of the chest; lower/farther portion of the left leg; inner side of the right ankle; back of the neck.

About 4 minutes

4. Treat abbreviations as controlled shorthand

EMS uses abbreviations, but abbreviations can be misunderstood. Learn the abbreviations used by your program and agency, write them clearly, and never guess when the meaning could change patient care.

  • Chief complaint (CC): the main reason the patient needs help, often stated in the patient’s own words.
  • Signs and symptoms (S/S): objective findings and subjective experiences.
  • History (Hx): past or current information relevant to the patient.
  • Loss of consciousness (LOC): a period when the patient was not conscious; context and duration matter.
  • Shortness of breath (SOB): commonly used, but plain language may be clearer in some communication.

Pronunciation also matters. Break long words into syllables, say them slowly, and verify unfamiliar terms using a trusted dictionary. Clear communication is more important than sounding impressive.

Deeper foundation

6. Notice how grammar changes medical words

Medical terms often come from Greek and Latin, so plural forms may not follow everyday English patterns. Examples include diagnosis/diagnoses, vertebra/vertebrae, and bronchus/bronchi. You do not need to memorize every plural before class, but recognize that an unfamiliar ending may indicate more than one structure.

Adjectives also change form. Abdomen is a noun; abdominal describes something related to the abdomen. Thorax becomes thoracic. Crane is not the same as cranial; careful spelling matters.

When reading a question, identify whether it asks for a structure, a process, a location, or a condition. The suffix often provides that clue. For example, -al often means “pertaining to,” while -itis suggests inflammation and -ectomy indicates surgical removal.

Communication practice

7. Translate between patient language and medical language

Patients may say “my heart is racing,” “I feel like I cannot get air,” or “my sugar is crashing.” The EMT listens to the patient’s own description, clarifies what it means, and then communicates findings precisely. Do not replace the patient’s words too early with a diagnosis.

Two-language practice

Patient statement: “The room started spinning when I stood up.” A clear clinical description might include sudden dizziness after standing, time of onset, associated nausea, balance, weakness, speech, and vital signs. It should not automatically become “stroke” or “vertigo” without assessment.

Good documentation separates quotation from interpretation. Use quotation marks for a meaningful exact statement and objective terms for what you observe.

Knowledge check · about 2 minutes

Check your understanding

Choose one answer for each question. This is a readiness check, not an EMT certification exam.

1. Which word part usually carries the main body-system meaning?

The root carries the central meaning, often a body structure or concept.

2. What does the prefix “hypo-” generally suggest?

Hypo- usually indicates low, below, or deficient.

3. “Distal to the elbow” means:

Distal means farther from the trunk or point of attachment.

4. Which practice is safest with an unfamiliar abbreviation?

Unclear abbreviations should be verified rather than guessed.

5. A useful first step when decoding a long medical term is to:

The suffix and root often reveal the type of term and its main meaning.
