First Day

Everyone remembers their first shift. Some people just wish they could forget parts of it.

CHAPTER 1

The New Guy

05:43 AM — Station 24

Tyler Brooks was forty-seven minutes early for his first shift. He had checked his uniform three times, polished his boots, and packed enough equipment to survive a small natural disaster. Two stethoscopes, trauma shears, a flashlight, three pens, a protocol book, an extra shirt, and a granola bar. He wasn't sure why he'd packed the granola bar. It just seemed like something an EMT should have.

Six months ago he'd been working at an auto parts store and taking EMT classes at night. Now he stood in a four-bay EMS station, staring at Medic 24.

“You moving in?” a voice asked. A gray-haired paramedic holding coffee nodded toward his backpack. “Planning on working an ambulance or climbing Everest?”

“Just wanted to be prepared. First day, sir.”

“Don't call me sir. Makes me feel old.”

“Yes, sir.”

The man closed his eyes. “Outstanding. Marcus Sullivan. Mac. I'm your field training officer.”

Mac counted off the rules: show up early, don't assume dispatch has the whole story, never say the word quiet, and never touch his coffee.

T.J. followed him toward the ambulance. His first real day in 911 EMS had begun.

CHAPTER 2

The Truck Check

06:02 AM

“Where's the oxygen?” Mac asked. T.J. identified the main cylinder and portable supply. “Suction?” T.J. found the mounted and portable units. “Pediatric BVM?” He hesitated, searched the airway cabinet, and finally held it up.

“Now check it,” Mac said. T.J. inspected the components and squeezed the bag. “Looks good.”

“Looks good, or works good?”

T.J. checked again. “Works good.”

They worked through airway supplies, trauma gear, glucometer, monitor, medications, and stretcher. Mac made him verify that equipment functioned, not just that it was present.

When T.J. nearly let the stretcher roll unexpectedly, Mac caught his eye. “That's why we practice before there's a patient on it. Don't apologize. Learn.”

Twenty minutes later T.J. was sweating. Mac checked his watch. “Breakfast?” The station tones sounded. “Never mind.”

CHAPTER 3

The First Call

06:49 AM

“Medic 24, respond to 1814 West Juniper Avenue. Fifty-eight-year-old male, unconscious, breathing. Diabetic history. Priority one.”

T.J. headed for the driver's door. Mac stopped him. “You're riding shotgun until I know you can drive this thing without removing somebody's mailbox.”

On the road, Mac asked what T.J. suspected. “Hypoglycemia? Maybe stroke, seizure, cardiac problem?”

“Good. Don't get tunnel vision. Start with scene safety and your ABCs. Don't let a monitor do your assessment for you.”

“Nervous?” Mac asked.

“A little.”

“Good. Means you understand this matters.”

At the house a worried woman met them. “Please hurry. My husband won't wake up.” Mac asked the man's name. “Robert.”

They followed her down a narrow hall. Robert lay on his side beside the bathtub, pale and sweating. The mannequin in EMT school had never looked like this.

CHAPTER 4

Twenty-Eight

06:57 AM

“T.J., you're up. Start your assessment,” Mac said calmly.

T.J. knelt beside Robert. “Sir? Can you hear me?” No response. He assessed the airway, breathing, and pulse. “He's breathing. Radial pulse present. Respirations eighteen. Cool, diaphoretic.”

“Good. Check his glucose.”

His hands fumbled with the test strips. “Slow is smooth, rookie,” Mac said. T.J. took a breath and finished the check. The meter beeped: 28 mg/dL.

“Twenty-eight,” he reported.

Mac asked Robert's wife about his insulin and breakfast while T.J. assisted with equipment and monitoring. Robert had taken insulin but hadn't eaten. Mac established IV access and treated him under their service protocol. They continued to reassess.

A few minutes later Robert opened his eyes. “Who are you? Why are you in my bathroom?”

“Your blood sugar was dangerously low,” Mac explained.

“Again?” Robert asked.

“Yes, again,” his wife said from the doorway.

“Can you take me somewhere she can't find me?”

T.J. laughed. Mac smiled. After reassessment and a discussion of risks, Robert agreed to transport. As they moved him to the stretcher, he studied T.J. “First day?”

“How'd you know?”

“You look more scared than I do.”

CHAPTER 5

The Report

08:14 AM — Red Mesa Regional

T.J. stared at the electronic patient care report. He'd written dozens of practice narratives. Now he couldn't remember the precise time of the first glucose check or how to organize the story.

Mac climbed into the cab. “How's the report?”

“Almost done.”

Mac glanced at the screen. “You've written three sentences.”

“Three really good sentences.”

Mac read: “Patient found on floor with low blood sugar. We fixed it and took him to hospital.” He handed the tablet back.

“What was his initial mental status? Airway? Respirations? Glucose? What did we do, and how did he respond?”

T.J. answered each question.

“Then write what you actually assessed, observed, treated, and reassessed. Use your notes and recorded times. Don't invent what you can't remember.”

Twenty minutes later, Mac reviewed the narrative. “Better. You were nervous on the call, but you slowed down and did your job.”

T.J. smiled.

“Don't get excited,” Mac added. “We still have ten hours left.”

CHAPTER 6

Welcome to 911

05:42 PM — Station 24

T.J. was exhausted. Since the diabetic call they'd handled a minor collision, a nursing-home fall, a psychiatric emergency, a breathing complaint, and a man who called 911 because he couldn't find his television remote. The remote had been underneath him.

He sat in the kitchen eating a vending-machine sandwich when Mac came in. “How was your first day?”

“Is it over?”

“Nope.”

“Does it ever get easier?”

Mac considered it. “Some parts. You learn the streets, the equipment, how to assess people without thinking through every step. But some calls stay with you. Some days you wonder whether you made the right decision.”

He glanced toward the bay. “Then there's a day when you make a difference. Sometimes it's big. Sometimes it's just being kind to someone having the worst day of their life.”

T.J. nodded.

“Enough inspirational nonsense,” Mac said. “Go restock the truck.”

T.J.'s phone vibrated. His mother: How was your first day? He thought about the fear, the paperwork, the jokes, and the people. He typed: Best day ever.

The tones sounded. “Medic 24, respond priority one. Reported cardiac arrest. CPR in progress.”

His stomach dropped. From the bay Mac called, “Come on, rookie!”

T.J. shoved his phone into his pocket and ran toward the ambulance. His first day wasn't over. Not even close.
