Lesson 1

HOW: Perform an abdominal assessment

Expose only as needed, preserve dignity, observe before touching, and palpate gently. Follow your local scope and protocols.

1

Ask first

Use OPQRST and SAMPLE to locate the complaint, clarify onset, identify associated symptoms, and learn about pregnancy possibility, surgery, medications, bleeding, vomiting, or bowel changes.

2

Position and expose

Place the patient comfortably, usually supine with knees relaxed when tolerated. Expose only the area needed and maintain warmth and privacy.

3

Inspect

Look for distention, bruising, scars, pulsation, masses, wounds, seat-belt marks, guarding, or asymmetry before palpation.

4

Palpate gently

Use light, systematic palpation. Begin away from the painful area and assess all quadrants. Stop if the patient develops severe pain or the exam could worsen an injury.

5

Describe the finding

Document location and whether the abdomen is soft, tender, rigid, distended, guarded, or associated with a palpable pulsating mass or trauma finding.

6

Connect and reassess

Combine abdominal findings with vitals, skin, mental status, bleeding, pregnancy status, mechanism, and pain pattern. Reassess after movement and during transport.

Do not repeatedly provoke pain. Your goal is to identify concerning findings and trends—not to reproduce every possible bedside test.

Lesson 2

WHY: Understand abdominal red flags

Abdominal emergencies can deteriorate before a clear diagnosis is available. EMT care focuses on recognition, supportive care, appropriate transport, and reassessment.

Localized tenderness

Pain and tenderness limited to one area may suggest a focal process, but location alone does not establish a diagnosis.

Guarding and rigidity

Voluntary guarding may occur from pain or fear. Involuntary rigidity is more concerning and should increase transport priority.

Distention

Distention may occur with bleeding, obstruction, pregnancy, ascites, gas, or organ enlargement. Interpret it with the overall presentation.

Internal bleeding

Abdominal trauma, GI bleeding, ectopic pregnancy, and vascular emergencies may produce shock with little or no visible blood loss.

Pulsating mass

A pulsating abdominal mass with pain, syncope, or poor perfusion is a high-risk finding. Avoid repeated palpation and expedite transport.

Trend and context

Vitals, skin signs, mental status, mechanism, medications, age, pregnancy possibility, and repeat exams often matter more than one isolated finding.
