How → Why → Practice

Decide whether the patient is breathing adequately—not just how fast.

Assess rate, depth, effort, rhythm, chest movement, speech, skin signs, and mental status. Then classify the finding, choose the appropriate EMT response, and document objectively.

0 of 3 lessons complete

Lesson 1

HOW: Assess breathing quality

Count respirations without announcing it, then describe the entire breathing pattern.

1

Observe before touching

Note position, ability to speak, alertness, visible distress, accessory-muscle use, retractions, nasal flaring, and tripod posture.

2

Count the rate

Count discreetly. Use a full minute when respirations are irregular, very slow, very fast, or difficult to see.

3

Assess depth and rhythm

Describe respirations as shallow, normal, or deep and as regular or irregular.

4

Inspect chest movement

Look for equal rise, symmetry, paradoxical motion, reduced movement, or fatigue.

5

Listen and feel

Listen for audible wheeze, stridor, grunting, or poor air movement. Auscultate lung sounds when indicated.

6

Judge adequacy

Combine breathing findings with skin signs, mental status, pulse, and oxygen saturation. Treat inadequate ventilation promptly.

Breathing-quality checklist

Rate + rhythm + depth + effort + chest rise + speech + air movement + perfusion + mental status.

Signs of adequate breathing

  • Appropriate rate for age and condition
  • Regular rhythm and adequate depth
  • Equal chest rise with minimal effort
  • Normal speech and mental status
  • Reasonable skin signs and oxygenation

Signs of inadequate breathing

  • Very slow, very fast, or irregular respirations
  • Shallow depth or poor chest rise
  • Exhaustion, decreasing effort, or altered mental status
  • Cyanosis, poor air movement, or inability to speak
  • Paradoxical movement or severe accessory-muscle use

Lesson 2

WHY: Understand ventilation and respiratory failure

A normal respiratory rate does not prove that ventilation is adequate. Depth, effort, and patient response matter.

Ventilation

Ventilation moves air into and out of the lungs. A patient can have an acceptable rate but inadequate tidal volume.

Oxygenation

Oxygenation describes oxygen transfer into the blood. SpO₂ helps, but it must be interpreted with the whole patient.

Compensation

Early distress may show tachypnea, anxiety, retractions, and accessory-muscle use while the patient maintains mental status.

Failure

Slowing respirations, decreasing effort, poor chest rise, altered mental status, and cyanosis may signal impending arrest.
