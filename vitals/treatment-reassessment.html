Integrated EMT Scenario

Treatment & Reassessment

EMT

Treat → Reassess → Revise → Document

Patient care does not stop after the first treatment.

Select the best immediate EMT action, predict what should change, review repeat findings, decide whether the patient improved, worsened, or stayed the same, and document the response objectively.

0 of 3 lessons complete

Lesson 1

HOW: Treat and reassess systematically

1

Identify the immediate threat

Prioritize airway, inadequate breathing, severe bleeding, poor perfusion, altered mental status, or another time-sensitive problem.

2

Choose one appropriate action

Select care that matches the finding, your EMT scope, local protocol, and the patient’s presentation.

3

State the expected response

Before reassessing, identify what improvement should look like: better air movement, rising glucose, improved mentation, controlled bleeding, or stronger perfusion.

4

Repeat the focused assessment

Recheck the finding that triggered treatment and repeat relevant vital signs. Use the same method whenever possible.

5

Compare before and after

Classify the response as improved, unchanged, or worsened. Do not rely only on how the patient says they feel.

6

Revise and continue care

Repeat or change treatment when appropriate, expedite transport when needed, and communicate meaningful changes.

Reassessment is treatment safety. A therapy that should help may have little effect, wear off, or reveal that the original impression was incomplete.

Lesson 2

WHY: Patient response is new assessment data

Improvement supports—but does not prove

A response can strengthen a working impression, but EMS should still avoid claiming a confirmed diagnosis.

No response matters

Unchanged findings may mean the treatment needs time, technique needs correction, or another cause is present.

Deterioration changes priority

Worsening airway, breathing, perfusion, or mental status requires immediate escalation and transport decisions.

Trends beat snapshots

Compare repeat measurements with the original values and note both the direction and speed of change.

Document timing

Record what was done, when it was done, the repeat findings, and whether additional care followed.

Use objective language

Write “SpO₂ increased from 88% to 95%” rather than only “patient improved.”
