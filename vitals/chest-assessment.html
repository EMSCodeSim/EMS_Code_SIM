Lesson 1

HOW: Perform a focused chest assessment

Expose only as needed, protect privacy and warmth, and treat immediate airway, breathing, and bleeding threats as they are found.

1

Observe the patient

Note position, speech, respiratory effort, skin signs, distress, and whether the patient can move air adequately.

2

Inspect the chest

Expose sufficiently to identify wounds, bruising, deformity, paradoxical movement, retractions, and unequal rise.

3

Compare movement

View and gently feel both sides for equal expansion. Identify delayed, reduced, or paradoxical movement.

4

Palpate carefully

Check for tenderness, instability, crepitus, subcutaneous emphysema, and hidden bleeding without repeatedly pressing an injury.

5

Assess breath sounds

Compare matching locations on both sides when trained and equipped. Relate sounds to effort, chest rise, SpO₂, and mental status.

6

Treat and reassess

Manage open wounds, support ventilation when inadequate, monitor closely, and repeat the chest assessment after interventions or change.

Do not delay care. Severe respiratory distress, inadequate ventilation, open chest wounds, major bleeding, or rapidly worsening findings require immediate treatment and transport.

Lesson 2

WHY: Understand what chest findings may mean

No single sign provides the diagnosis. Combine chest findings with mechanism, history, vitals, oxygenation, perfusion, and trends.

Unequal chest rise

May reflect splinting, pneumothorax, hemothorax, airway obstruction, mainstem ventilation, or another unilateral problem.

Paradoxical movement

A segment moving opposite the rest of the chest suggests major chest-wall instability and possible underlying lung injury.

Open chest wound

Air may move through the chest wall and interfere with normal ventilation. Follow local protocol for appropriate sealing and frequent reassessment.

Crepitus or instability

Can indicate rib or sternum injury. Avoid repeated palpation and watch for worsening ventilation and shock.

Absent or reduced sounds

May be caused by poor air movement, obstruction, pneumothorax, hemothorax, anatomy, or technique. Recheck and compare sides.

Normal-looking chest

A patient can still have serious respiratory or internal injury. Continue the full assessment and trend the patient over time.
