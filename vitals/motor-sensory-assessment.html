Lesson 1

HOW: Perform a focused motor and sensory assessment

Explain each request, compare left with right, protect injured extremities, and repeat the exam after movement, splinting, or a change in condition.

1

Establish baseline

Confirm level of consciousness and orientation. Ask about weakness, numbness, tingling, pain, headache, trauma, or the exact time symptoms began.

2

Observe face and speech

Ask the patient to smile or show teeth. Listen for slurred, inappropriate, or absent speech and compare facial movement on both sides.

3

Test upper extremities

Assess arm drift when appropriate, compare hand grips, and observe whether each arm moves equally. Do not force movement through suspected injury.

4

Test lower extremities

Compare movement and strength with foot pushes and pulls or another approved method. Note inability, weakness, pain limitation, or asymmetry.

5

Check sensation and distal PMS

Compare light-touch sensation side to side. For injured limbs, document distal pulse, motor function, and sensation before and after splinting.

6

Localize, trend, and report

Determine whether the pattern is focal, generalized, or isolated to an injured limb. Record last-known-well time when stroke is possible and reassess during transport.

Compare sides. A subtle difference between left and right may be more important than the absolute strength of either side.

Lesson 2

WHY: Understand abnormal motor and sensory patterns

Weakness or sensory loss may arise from stroke, spinal injury, limb trauma, metabolic illness, intoxication, or generalized critical illness.

Focal deficit

One-sided facial droop, arm weakness, sensory loss, or speech change is concerning for a focal neurologic emergency such as stroke.

Generalized weakness

Symmetric weakness may occur with hypoglycemia, infection, dehydration, medication effects, toxic exposure, or other systemic illness.

Spinal pattern

Weakness or sensory change below a suspected spinal injury, especially when bilateral, is a high-risk finding requiring careful movement and rapid transport.

Distal neurovascular compromise

Absent pulse, worsening numbness, or inability to move distal to an injury may indicate limb-threatening compromise. Reassess before and after splinting.

Pain-limited movement

A patient may not move because of pain rather than neurologic loss. Document what was attempted, what limited the exam, and whether sensation and pulse remain intact.

Time matters

When stroke is possible, document the last time the patient was known normal and communicate the finding early so the receiving system can prepare.
