Lesson 1

HOW: Perform a rapid trauma exam

Address immediate threats first, then examine from head to toe using the same sequence every time.

1

Control major bleeding

Look immediately for severe external hemorrhage. Apply direct pressure, wound packing, or a tourniquet according to protocol.

2

Assess head and neck

Inspect and palpate for DCAP-BTLS, blood or fluid, facial instability, airway threats, tracheal position, and jugular venous distention when appropriate.

3

Assess the chest

Expose as needed. Inspect, palpate, and listen for equal rise, tenderness, crepitus, open wounds, paradoxical motion, and unequal breath sounds.

4

Assess abdomen and pelvis

Check for tenderness, guarding, rigidity, distention, bruising, instability, and signs of internal bleeding. Avoid repeated pelvic compression.

5

Assess extremities

Inspect and palpate each limb for deformity, wounds, tenderness, swelling, and instability. Check distal pulse, motor function, and sensation.

6

Assess posterior and reassess

Inspect the back when movement is safe, maintain spinal precautions when indicated, trend vital signs, and repeat the primary assessment.

Use DCAP-BTLS

Deformities, Contusions, Abrasions, Punctures/Penetrations, Burns, Tenderness, Lacerations, Swelling.

Objective exam language

  • Location and side
  • Size and appearance
  • Tenderness or instability
  • Bleeding amount and control method
  • Distal pulse, motor, and sensation

Immediate threats

  • Uncontrolled hemorrhage
  • Airway or ventilation compromise
  • Open or unstable chest injury
  • Signs of shock or internal bleeding
  • New neurologic or neurovascular deficit

Lesson 2

WHY: Link trauma findings to patient priority

The exam is not a search for every injury before transport. Its purpose is to identify threats that change immediate care and destination decisions.

Mechanism guides—not proves

Mechanism raises suspicion, but treatment should be based on the patient’s actual presentation and assessment findings.

Shock can be hidden

Compensated patients may maintain blood pressure while showing tachycardia, cool skin, anxiety, delayed capillary refill, or narrowing pulse pressure.

Trends matter

Repeated vital signs and mental-status checks may reveal deterioration that was not obvious during the first exam.

PMS protects limbs

Pulse, motor function, and sensation should be documented before and after splinting or movement whenever possible.
